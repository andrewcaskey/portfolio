# Get Into It

**Date:** May 22, 2026 · **Category:** Entrepreneurship

Look around you. What needs to be fixed? Get into it.

That's the whole framework. Everything else is execution.

---

## Problems First, Empire Second

The entrepreneurial instinct that actually works isn't "I want to build something massive." It's "I want to solve problems for people." Those sound similar but they produce completely different businesses.

The first is ego-driven — you're building toward a vision of yourself. The second is market-driven — you're building toward what people actually need. The market one wins, almost always.

The end consumer is a human. Understanding that consumer requires taste — and taste can only come from a human. You can have a technically perfect foundation and still produce something nobody connects with. Songs are the clearest example. A track can be sonically flawless and still be forgettable. What makes it land is the taste layered on top.

The businesses worth building reduce friction. They make life easier for the humans who use them. Over time, enough of those improvements compound into a genuinely better world — higher living conditions, more time, more capacity for people to do things that matter to them. That's not idealism. That's just what useful products do at scale.

---

## How to Actually Get Into It

You don't start by asking "what should I build?" You start by looking around.

What frustrates you in your daily work? What do you patch with a manual workaround every week? What does your team complain about repeatedly? Those are your candidates. Pick the one where you have the most insight — the problem you understand better than most people because you live inside it.

Then show people how to solve it. That's the whole move. You don't need a product yet. You need to be the person who understands the problem so well that you can walk someone through a solution.

There's a version of this that's straightforward: if you understand how to apply AI tools to business workflows, you can earn serious money just by showing businesses what's possible and setting it up for them. The value isn't in the technology — it's in knowing what to do with it. That knowledge is scarcer than you'd think.

---

## The Balance Problem

The hardest thing about building something is staying whole while you do it.

Hyperfocus is the gift and the trap. When you're deep in it, you can ship things that shouldn't be possible. You can solve problems in a weekend that your competitors spend quarters on. But hyperfocus is expensive. It costs health, relationships, and the long view. You lose sight of what you said you were building toward in the first place.

I know a founder — call him John — who built something genuinely impressive. He also gained 80 pounds three times over the course of doing it. And 100 pounds during a divorce. When he stopped working, the weight came off almost immediately. His body was keeping score. He badly wanted to win for everybody around him, for his employees and his customers and his family. He was willing to lose himself in the process.

That's a cautionary note, not a success story.

---

## The Annual Personal Plan

The fix isn't balance — that's not realistic when you're building. The fix is intentionality about what you're willing to sacrifice and what you're not.

Once a year, answer three questions:

**1. The one area of my life I'm most willing to let slip while building.**

Pick one. Not five. Naming it honestly means you've chosen it, which is different from it being taken from you by default.

**2. The specific, small behavior that would protect the area I'm not willing to sacrifice.**

Not a resolution. A behavior. "Walk every morning" is a behavior. "Take care of my health" is not. Small and concrete enough that you either did it or you didn't.

**3. What winning looks like for me personally — not just professionally — twelve months from now.**

This one matters most. If your definition of winning is only a revenue number or a product milestone, you will optimize for exactly that and nothing else. The professional and personal have to share a definition of success, or one will consume the other.

---

Write it down. Review it quarterly. The goal isn't to get it right — it's to keep asking the question. The people who ask it regularly tend to still be in the game five years later. The ones who don't tend to win some sprint and then disappear.

Look around you. What needs to be fixed? Get into it — and keep the rest of your life alive while you do.
